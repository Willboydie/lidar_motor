For our application we set $V_d = 0$ for simplicity (a good assumption at slow speeds).

We calculate $V_q$ as:

$$
V_q = I \cdot R_{\mathrm{phase}} + V_{\mathrm{bemf}}
$$

where:

$$
V_{\mathrm{bemf}} = \frac{\omega_{\mathrm{shaft}}}{K_v \cdot \sqrt{3} \cdot \mathrm{RPMTORADS}}
$$

# Explanation of FOC Control

An $N$ pole-pair, 3-PWM BLDC motor has a stator with three coils around the outside and a solid magnet rotor with $2N$ “pizza slices,” or poles (N, S, …).

Each pole has a pair, so in our case we have a magnet pizza with 14 slices, made up of 7 opposing pole pairs.

At any one time we can control the current through each of the coils independently. The current through a coil creates a magnetic force in the direction that coil faces (see [this great video](https://youtu.be/PgM8zWJr8-U?si=xZ6-wqovyPyhzzHs)).

So we have three force vectors acting on the magnet pizza at any one time.

The direction of the force on the magnet at any one time depends not only on the currents in each coil but also on the angular position of the pizza. More specifically, the angle that matters is the angle of the magnetic field.

Because the pizza essentially repeats its North, South, North, South pattern $N$ times, magnetic north repeats itself $N$ times in one full mechanical rotation.

So, to repeat: the force on the rotor (the pizza magnet) at any one time is determined by:

1. The angle of magnetic north of the pizza relative to the direction of the magnetic force generated by the three coils
2. The magnitude of each force vector (i.e. the current in each coil)

## Naming

- $I_a$, $I_b$, $I_c$ — coil currents
- $\theta_e$ — the angle between the magnetic fields, known as the **electrical angle**

## Control flow

If we know the electrical angle and wish to generate a desired force vector, we can use the current electrical angle along with the **inverse Park and Clarke transforms** to convert the desired force vector into the desired coil currents.

If we know the coil currents via current sensing, we can use PID control to decide the desired voltage.

We use PWM to communicate the desired voltages to the DRV8313.

# PID

The force vector we wish to generate will be based on the velocity, angle, or torque we wish to achieve.

If, for instance, we wish to achieve a certain mechanical angle, knowing our current one, we can use PID control to estimate the desired force vector.

The PID controller takes the error over time as an input and calculates:

- The **derivative** of that error at any one time (for a correction damping effect)
- The **integral** of the error over time (for a bias correction effect)

The output of the PID controller is:

$$
\mathrm{output} = P \cdot e + I \int e  \mathrm{d}t + D \frac{\mathrm{d}e}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

where $P$, $I$, and $D$ are constants: the proportional, integral, and derivative gains respectively.
